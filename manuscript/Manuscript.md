# An empirically anchored multi-study rhizosphere 16S rRNA benchmark reveals method-specific failure boundaries and limits to cross-project generalization

## Abstract

Public rhizosphere microbiome datasets vary across crops, locations, amplicon regions and analytical workflows, yet the reliability of commonly used methods under this heterogeneity remains insufficiently characterized. We integrated 45 maize, rice and wheat rhizosphere 16S rRNA projects comprising 1,219 samples and 2,025 genus- or family-level features, including one unclassified aggregate. We developed a simulation benchmark with known ground truth, anchored to observed project-level structure and calibrated using eight structural summaries. We evaluated differential abundance analysis, core-taxon identification and network inference, together with a complementary genus-level sequence classification benchmark, and assessed the structural transportability of the generators using repeated project-level holdouts.

Differential-abundance performance was scenario dependent. In the strongest sparse-effect scenario under the template-based generator, ANCOM-BC2 achieved a mean per-dataset FDP of 0.008, power of 0.878 and F1 of 0.930, whereas LinDA had a mean FDP of 0.353. Under the correlated-perturbation generator, ANCOM-BC2 retained the highest F1 score, while LinDA had an FDP of 0.257. Project-balanced prevalence achieved the highest mean F1 for core-taxon identification (0.859), although all methods deteriorated under depth confounding (F1 0.423–0.497). Network methods showed limited recovery of direct edges, with mean areas under the precision-recall curve of 0.065–0.088 in the synthetic validation scenarios. Stability across real-data panels did not imply greater accuracy against synthetic ground truth. In closed-set sequence classification, the linear k-mer model achieved an accuracy of 0.915, compared with 0.090 and 0.075 for a small one-dimensional convolutional neural network and a k-mer Transformer trained from scratch, respectively. Rejection of previously unseen genera remained challenging.

Structural calibration across three project panels generally met the prespecified tolerances for seven or eight of the eight summary measures. However, across 30 held-out project panels generated by 10 repetitions of stratified three-fold splitting, the template-based and correlated-perturbation generators met these tolerances for only 3.87 and 3.40 measures on average, respectively. Empirical predictive coverage was 0.033 for network centrality and ranged from 0.167 to 0.200 for network density. The held-out analysis therefore evaluated the structural transportability of the generators rather than directly validating method accuracy on real projects. These findings support scenario-specific method comparisons and explicit characterization of failure conditions, but they do not establish a universally applicable method ranking or reliable structural generalization to previously unseen rhizosphere projects.

**Keywords:** rhizosphere microbiome; 16S rRNA; differential abundance; core microbiome; co-occurrence network; taxonomic classification; structural transportability; reproducibility

## Introduction

Rhizosphere 16S studies increasingly seek general statements about crop-associated bacteria from collections of public datasets. Such reuse introduces a form of evidence synthesis that differs from a conventional meta-analysis: reads or features are reprocessed under a common pipeline, project-level differences may dominate treatment contrasts, and the resulting operational taxonomic units or amplicon sequence variants are sensitive to primer region and reference database [1–4]. The same compositional data may also be analyzed for several scientific objectives, including differential abundance, core membership, co-occurrence structure and taxonomic prediction. These objectives use different truth definitions and failure modes, so a method that performs well in one domain cannot be assumed to perform well in another.

Method benchmarking in microbiome research has revealed discordant differential-abundance results [5–7]. Compositional transformations, zero handling, depth effects and dependence among taxa can change both false-discovery control and power. Template-based simulation reduces the distance between null data and real study structure [7], but a simulator fitted to one collection of projects can encode the idiosyncrasies of that collection. Calibration against aggregate summaries is therefore necessary but is not equivalent to structural transportability to unseen project panels.

Core microbiome analysis and network inference pose additional inferential problems. A core feature may be defined by prevalence, abundance or latent detection probability, and these definitions are not interchangeable [15]. Co-occurrence edges can reflect shared environment, compositionality, depth or indirect associations; they are statistical relationships rather than demonstrated ecological interactions [12,16]. Sequence classifiers add a separate source of error through reference incompleteness and unseen taxa [3]. Thus, a multi-domain benchmark should combine calibration, known truth, robustness across generators and structural checks on held-out project panels.

We developed a benchmark around a harmonized collection of 45 public rhizosphere projects from three cereals. A project panel is defined as a crop-stratified set of projects used as one evaluation unit; repeated holdout assigns complete project panels to evaluation while retaining the remaining projects for fitting or donor matching. The study asked four questions. First, do common differential-abundance methods preserve FDP and power under frozen sparse and dense effect scenarios? Second, how accurately do prevalence, empirical-Bayes and occupancy models recover a latent project-level core? Third, do correlation, proportionality and partial-correlation estimators recover direct network edges, and are their synthetic rankings consistent with cross-panel real-data stability? Fourth, do classical k-mer classifiers and small neural models recover genus labels and reject unseen genera? We then assessed whether the generators transported structurally to held-out project panels. The benchmark distinguishes internal calibration, simulation-based method evaluation, structural transportability and reliability on real-project outcomes rather than collapsing them into a single ranking.

## Results

The four analysis modules do not share a single estimand. Table 5 therefore defines each ground truth, the quantity estimated by the candidate methods and the boundary of the corresponding evaluation before the module-specific results are presented.

### The benchmark represents a heterogeneous multi-project resource

The harmonized resource contained 45 projects, with 15 projects and 391–431 samples per crop (Fig. 1a,b; Table 1). Project sample size ranged from 4 to 35 samples and had a median of 30. All 45 projects were reprocessed from public FASTQ reads rather than assembled from published abundance tables. After filtering, the shared feature space contained 1,580 genus bins, 444 family-level fallback bins and one unassigned bin. The complete matrix contained 1,219 samples and 2,025 features; 2,024 represented biological labels and one represented reads not assignable to an eligible genus or family. After requiring at least 1,000 bacterial reads, 1,192 samples were used for composition-based parameter estimation.

Sparsity and technical heterogeneity were substantial. The full matrix had a zero fraction of 91.8%; only 53 features were observed in at least 50% of samples and seven in at least 70%. Project-level median bacterial depth ranged from 1,863 to 45,530 reads, modal ASV length ranged from 249 to 482 bp, and genus-level read assignment ranged from 31.2% to 90.8% across projects (Table 1). The unassigned bin accounted for 16.3% of bacterial reads overall, while family fallback bins accounted for 19.7%. These properties motivated a simulator that preserved whole sample templates, project membership, depth and zero structure rather than drawing each feature independently.

The primary template-multinomial generator met the prespecified tolerance for all eight structural calibration summaries (Fig. 1d). Distances from the observed summaries were 0.0077 for the prevalence distribution, 0.0080 for rank abundance, 0.0000 for depth, 0.0029 for the within/between-project variance ratio, 0.0021 for Bray–Curtis distances, 0.0051 for Aitchison distances, 0.0089 for zero fraction and 0.1273 for network topology. These are operational distances, not independent statistical tests. Removing the unassigned bin changed the compositional denominator but all eight distances remained within tolerance (Supplementary Table S6). The no-effect differential-abundance grid produced zero false discoveries across all four methods and 20 datasets.

The executed design comprised 100 primary-generator differential-abundance datasets, 25 correlated-perturbation datasets, 100 core datasets, 40 network datasets across calibration and validation seeds, five classification split seeds and 30 repeated held-out project panels (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Table S1). The workflow therefore evaluated structure-preserving scenarios and, separately, whether fitted structure transferred to unseen project panels.

### Differential-abundance ranking is scenario specific and LinDA has a reproducible sparse-effect boundary

The four methods showed a large separation in the strong sparse-effect scenario, where 20 of 120 tested features had true absolute log2 effects of 1.00. Under generator 1, ANCOM-BC2 achieved a mean per-dataset FDP of 0.008, power of 0.878 and F1 of 0.930 (Fig. 2a,b,c; Table 2; Supplementary Table S3). ALDEx2 was conservative, with mean FDP 0.000 but power 0.150. LinDA had mean FDP 0.353, power 0.220 and F1 0.326, while MaAsLin2 had FDP 0.048, power 0.275 and F1 0.423.

The LinDA result was not confined to one seed block. The three reported values describe the same strong sparse-effect scenario: 0.363 is the mean across primary seeds 4000–4009, 0.343 is the mean across extension seeds 4010–4019 and 0.353 is the mean across all 20 seeds. These are split-half summaries, not three independent experiments. All primary values and eight of ten extension values exceeded 0.20 (Fig. 2d). Two false-positive features were detected in all 10 extension seeds, indicating a reproducible model-boundary response rather than isolated Monte Carlo variation. The pooled FDP across all rejections was 0.348, consistent with the mean of finite per-dataset values (Supplementary Table S3). A Benjamini–Yekutieli sensitivity analysis removed the observed false discoveries but reduced discovery counts, consistent with a stricter error criterion rather than improved target recovery.

The ranking weakened when effects became smaller or the truth structure became denser. ANCOM-BC2 remained the highest-powered method, but power fell to 0.045 and 0.140 in the two smaller sparse-effect scenarios and to 0.136 in the 40-target dense scenario. LinDA and MaAsLin2 made no discoveries in the two smaller sparse scenarios; ALDEx2 had power at or below 0.009 outside the strongest sparse scenario. The no-effect control produced no rejections for any method (Supplementary Table S2).

Generator 2 perturbed the same calibrated template through correlated multiplicative changes and therefore represents a semi-parametric second family rather than an independent ecological simulator. In its strong sparse-effect scenario, ANCOM-BC2 again ranked highest, with mean FDP 0.000, power 0.840 and F1 0.913 (Table 2). LinDA again showed elevated FDP (0.257; pooled FDP 0.255) and higher power than in generator 1 (0.350). MaAsLin2 had FDP 0.047 and power 0.400. The direction of the main ranking was therefore reproduced, while absolute method performance differed between generators.

### Core recovery collapses under depth confounding

Here, core status denotes a latent property of the simulation: a feature is core when its detection probability is at least 0.90 in at least 80% of projects. It is not defined by the observed global prevalence of a particular feature. The prevalence and occupancy models therefore estimate related but non-identical quantities, and disagreements among them are interpreted against this explicit latent definition.

Across the four non-null scenarios, project-balanced prevalence achieved the highest mean core F1 (0.859), followed by hierarchical occupancy detection (0.833), pooled sample prevalence (0.804) and beta-binomial empirical Bayes (0.752) (Fig. 3b; Table 3). All methods made no false core calls in the null scenario. Their worst scenario-specific false-core rates ranged from 0.038 to 0.076.

Performance was high in balanced, low-abundance and project-shift scenarios. Project-balanced prevalence reached F1 0.990, 0.987 and 0.964, respectively, while hierarchical occupancy reached 0.975, 0.926 and 0.958. The low-abundance scenario confirmed that a rare taxon can satisfy a latent occupancy definition; methods were not uniformly dependent on high mean abundance. The null and balanced scenarios also showed that observed prevalence and the latent project-level definition agree closely when detection is not strongly depth dependent.

The common failure boundary was depth confounding. In that scenario, mean F1 fell to 0.497 for project-balanced prevalence, 0.472 for hierarchical occupancy, 0.450 for sample prevalence and 0.423 for beta-binomial empirical Bayes (Fig. 3a,d). The project-balanced method retained recall 0.752 but precision only 0.387; hierarchical occupancy had recall 0.669 and precision 0.380. Thus the depth-confounded state produced both missed core taxa and false core calls and should not be averaged away when reporting core-method performance.

The first-ranked method requires a specific interpretation. Project-balanced prevalence mirrors the project-level population-core definition more directly than the alternatives and was informed by real-data exploratory work before the formal grid. Its advantage is therefore partly definitional. It should be regarded as a strong transparent baseline for this core definition, not as evidence that prevalence thresholds recover an invariant biological core.

### Synthetic network accuracy and real-panel stability identify different methods

All network methods recovered direct edges weakly in the synthetic Gaussian graph benchmark. Across non-null validation scenarios, CLR Spearman had the highest mean edge AUPRC (0.088), followed by CLR Pearson (0.072), graphical lasso (0.069) and the SparCC-like estimator (0.065); mean hub-degree Spearman correlations were close to zero (Fig. 4a; Table 4). These values must be interpreted against edge prevalence. Among 780 candidate edges, the sparse and project-shift scenarios contained approximately 25.8 true edges on average (3.31%), giving a random-ranking AUPRC of 0.033, whereas the dense scenario contained 73.6 true edges (9.44%), giving a random baseline of 0.094 (Supplementary Table S4). The observed improvements over random were therefore modest, particularly for SparCC-like in the sparse and project-shift settings. The top-decile score cutoff created a network with 3.9 edges per node even when the true graph had no edges, demonstrating that an apparently dense co-occurrence network can be generated by a fixed correlation cutoff.

Real three-panel stability led to a different ordering. The SparCC-like estimator had the highest pairwise edge Jaccard overlap (0.188) and hub-degree Spearman correlation (0.617), followed by CLR Pearson (0.136 and 0.239), CLR Spearman (0.132 and 0.271) and graphical lasso (0.085 and 0.431) (Fig. 4b). The method with the best synthetic direct-edge AUPRC, CLR Spearman, did not have the greatest cross-panel stability, while the most stable method was not the most accurate against synthetic direct edges.

These endpoints answer different questions. Here, the synthetic truth is the set of non-zero off-diagonal entries of a Gaussian precision matrix, which represents conditional dependence under the simulator. AUPRC asks whether an estimator prioritizes these known direct edges in a controlled graph. Cross-panel Jaccard asks whether the estimator produces similar high-scoring edges when project composition changes. Marginal correlation methods and conditional-dependence methods do not estimate an identical target, so their ranking is partly a function of estimand alignment. Neither endpoint validates ecological interaction.

### Classical k-mer classifiers outperform the tested small from-scratch models under a limited training budget

Five independent splits of a SILVA r138.2 V4 genus benchmark compared classical and neural sequence classifiers. The linear SGD classifier achieved the highest closed-set accuracy (0.915 ± 0.029) and macro F1 (0.905 ± 0.032), followed by k-mer nearest neighbors (0.870 and 0.847) and multinomial naive Bayes (0.855 and 0.829) (Fig. 4c; Table 4; Supplementary Table S5). A small one-dimensional CNN achieved accuracy 0.090 and macro F1 0.030, while the from-scratch k-mer Transformer achieved 0.075 and 0.031. The balanced majority-class baseline was 0.05 accuracy and 0.0025 macro F1.

The deep models were deliberately small and were trained for four epochs, corresponding to approximately 12 optimization steps per split, without class weighting. Training and test queries were derived from disjoint source sequences, but the design did not cluster near-duplicate reference sequences before splitting. These choices leave open the possibility of near-neighbor leakage and do not establish that a well-tuned neural network would perform poorly. The classification experiment is therefore a complementary reference-set analysis, separate from the eight-summary project-structure simulation.

Under unseen-genus shift, multinomial naive Bayes rejected the largest fraction of unknown queries (0.490), followed by linear SGD (0.455) and k-mer nearest neighbors (0.295). The threshold was calibrated to retain 95% of known validation queries. At this operating point, known-taxon retention during the unseen-genera evaluation was 0.95 for every method, while the CNN and Transformer rejected only 0.085 and 0.060 of unseen genera. The result supports a limited conclusion: under the evaluated data splits, tokenization choices and training budget, the linear k-mer model outperformed the two neural models trained from scratch. It does not establish a general advantage of linear models over deep learning for 16S classification.

### Repeated project-panel holdout reveals limited structural transportability

Three disjoint 15-project panels represented all 45 projects, with five projects per crop in each panel. Structural calibration was consistently strong: the six panel-by-generator combinations passed 7.33–8.00 of eight summaries on average, with a minimum of seven checks in every combination (Fig. 5a). Network topology was the recurring borderline summary.

This within-panel structural result did not transfer to held-out project panels. Ten repeated stratified three-fold partitions yielded 30 held-out panels. Using only training-project information and held-out project count, crop and depth, the template generator met 3.87 of eight fixed-tolerance summaries on average, and the correlated-perturbation generator met 3.40 (Fig. 5b). Pooled distances were largest for network topology, zero fraction, prevalence and Aitchison composition. Because every project entered one held-out panel in each partition and therefore appeared exactly 10 times per generator, the 30 panels represent correlated repeated evaluations rather than 30 independent external cohorts. We report their distribution descriptively and do not apply independent-sample confidence intervals or significance tests to the panel count.

Empirical predictive-interval coverage showed the same pattern. Depth coverage was 1.000 because holdout depths were supplied by design. For the template generator, coverage was 0.567 for prevalence and rank abundance, 0.533 for Bray–Curtis, 0.433 for the variance ratio, 0.333 for Aitchison distance, 0.167 for network density and 0.033 for network centrality. For the correlated-perturbation generator, prevalence, Bray–Curtis, Aitchison, variance ratio and zero fraction had coverage between 0.433 and 0.467, network density had coverage 0.200 and network centrality coverage 0.033 (Fig. 5c).

The intervals used five predictive draws per held-out fold in the completed repeated run, so coverage is a coarse diagnostic rather than a precise estimate of a nominal 95% interval. Even with that qualification, the shortfall was consistent across partitions and both generators. Project-level structural calibration therefore cannot be used as evidence that the simulator can predict unseen projects. Importantly, this analysis does not estimate the accuracy, FDP or ranking of the analysis methods on real held-out projects, because those outcomes lack complete real-world ground truth. The supported conclusion is narrower: the current projection model does not transport structurally from the available project metadata alone.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This benchmark produced four results. First, differential-abundance performance was strongly scenario dependent. ANCOM-BC2 was the strongest tested method in the frozen grid, but its advantage was driven primarily by the strong sparse and dense scenarios, and its power remained modest under smaller effects. LinDA exhibited reproducible FDP inflation when 20 target features carried large effects in a sparse matrix. Second, core recovery depended more on the relationship between occupancy and sequencing depth than on the choice among high-performing prevalence and occupancy models. Third, synthetic network truth recovery and real cross-panel stability were distinct endpoints and led to different rankings. Fourth, under the tested splits and limited neural training budget, established k-mer classifiers performed better than the two small from-scratch neural models on the V4 reference task.

The cross-domain pattern is more useful than any single winner. A method can preserve FDP yet have low power, recover a core under balanced occupancy yet fail under depth confounding, rank edges well in a synthetic graph yet be unstable across real panels, or classify known genera accurately yet reject unseen genera poorly. Reporting one global score would obscure these boundaries.

### Why limited structural transportability matters

Three-panel calibration showed that the simulator reproduced the structure of panels drawn from the same 45-project resource. It did not test prediction to a panel excluded from fitting. The repeated holdout addressed that stronger structural question by withholding a complete 15-project panel, fitting or matching from the remaining 30 projects, and evaluating nine structural summaries without access to held-out composition. It still did not test method accuracy, FDP or ranking against real held-out truth, which is unavailable for these projects.

The 30 held-out panels are correlated because projects recur across split repetitions. Their means therefore describe partition-to-partition behavior rather than 30 independent external cohorts. Even with that restriction, the shortfall recurred across partitions and both generators, with mean pass counts ranging from 3.00 to 4.67 per partition for the template generator and from 2.33 to 4.67 for the correlated-perturbation generator. Network centrality and density were especially problematic, which is consistent with the sensitivity of network estimates to project composition. Simulator-based rankings in this study remain conditional on the fitted project panel and target envelope.

This result also clarifies what would be needed before a strong structural transportability claim: explicit modeling of crop and project-panel random effects, richer environmental and technical covariates, more diverse training panels and an independently designed ecological generator that passes holdout without access to target compositions. Adding more seeds alone would not repair model misspecification.

### Implications for rhizosphere method selection

For an analysis containing many zero-count features and a subset of strong within-project effects, the benchmark supports ANCOM-BC2 as a reasonable first candidate while requiring sensitivity analysis. LinDA should be used cautiously in comparable sparse, strong-effect settings because its FDP inflation replicated across seed stages and a correlated generator. ALDEx2 was conservative in this grid; its low discovery rate is not evidence of universal inferiority because the result depends on effect-size distribution and aggregation choices. MaAsLin2 occupied an intermediate position, with controlled FDP and moderate power in the strongest scenario.

This ranking should not be converted into a rule that the most complex model is always preferable. Elementary or transparent transformations can show greater replicability in other differential-abundance settings [17]. The appropriate response to method sensitivity is to pre-specify a primary analysis, report alternative estimators and make the failure boundary visible.

For core identification, project-balanced prevalence is a defensible transparent baseline when the scientific definition is explicitly project-level. That interpretation must be stated in advance. When depth is associated with occupancy, no tested method should be treated as providing a definitive core list. Proposed core features should be reported with depth sensitivity and with uncertainty about detection rather than as a fixed biological entity.

For network analysis, the benchmark supports reporting both synthetic truth recovery and real-data stability. A stable edge is not necessarily a direct edge, and a high AUPRC in a Gaussian graph does not establish that the same estimator captures microbial interactions. Top-decile edges can appear in null data, so thresholds should be calibrated against a null distribution and interpreted as co-occurrence scores.

For sequence classification, the current evidence supports sparse linear and nearest-neighbor baselines under the evaluated small-reference protocol. The performance of the from-scratch CNN and Transformer should not be generalized to well-tuned neural networks or pretrained genomic language models such as DNABERT [19]. A fair DNA-BERT evaluation requires a fixed pretrained encoder, domain-specific tokenization, adequate fine-tuning and a split based on sequence-similarity clusters to prevent reference leakage.

### Evidence components and their limits

The study contributes four connected components of evidence: observed project-level structure, simulation calibration, method evaluation against known ground truth and structural validation on held-out project panels. Execution validity was complete for the formal DA jobs and core method metrics. The null simulator met all eight operational tolerances, and the core grid passed every stage-by-scenario structural calibration cell. Internal robustness included primary and extension seeds, a correlated second generator, 20 core datasets per scenario, disjoint network validation seeds and five classification splits.

The fourth component tests structural transportability, not method accuracy on real projects. Its limited support constrains extrapolation from the simulated rankings. A reader may use the benchmark to choose a sensitivity-analysis set or to anticipate a failure boundary. A reader should not use it to claim that one method is universally superior across crops, soils, primer regions or study designs.

## Limitations

The simulated effects are abundance shifts delivered through a sample-template multinomial model. Prevalence-only effects, taxon–taxon interactions, treatment assigned only at the project level and time-varying effects were not included. The target estimand is the expected post-renormalization change in a feature’s log2 relative abundance, not an absolute abundance change. The correlated perturbation generator shares the fitted template with generator 1 and is not a fully independent ecological generator. An attempted hurdle-Dirichlet generator was not promoted because it failed the same holdout criteria.

The network benchmark uses a Gaussian precision-matrix truth. This gives a controlled conditional-dependence target but simplifies non-Gaussian ecological dependence, indirect effects and higher-order associations. Marginal CLR correlation and SparCC-like proportionality do not estimate the same target as graphical lasso, so part of the method contrast reflects estimand alignment. The implementation labeled SparCC-like uses ten iterative proportionality updates and is an approximation, not the complete SparCC software. The real-panel stability analysis measures reproducibility across three panels, not correspondence to ecological interactions.

The classification benchmark uses one SILVA r138.2 V4 extract, 24 eligible genera and simulated sequence substitutions. It does not evaluate primer-region shift, conflicting reference taxonomy, chimeras, full-length references or a pretrained DNA-BERT model. Source sequences assigned to training, validation and test sets were disjoint, but no sequence-similarity clustering was performed before splitting, leaving a risk of near-duplicate leakage across sets. The neural baselines used four epochs, no class weighting and a small parameter budget; they should not be interpreted as representative benchmarks of modern deep learning.

The differential-abundance study uses a fixed 15-project panel and a 120-feature analysis panel per scenario. The primary and extension stages reuse the same overall study frame, and the second generator preserves that frame. The all-project structural validation is designed to expose this limitation, but the current simulator does not pass it. Projects also differ in sample size, sequencing depth, primer region, extraction protocol, platform, geography and rhizosphere sampling definition. Crop and water-regime variables are partially collinear in the source resource, so no causal crop or management effect is inferred. Crop stratification should therefore be interpreted as design balance, not as elimination of technical confounding.

The core benchmark's project-balanced prevalence method closely mirrors the population-core truth definition. Its advantage is therefore partly definitional. The depth-confounded scenario is a stress test, not an estimate of the prevalence of depth confounding in all rhizosphere studies. Detection failure cannot be distinguished from absence in real data without occupancy modeling or additional metadata.

The repeated holdout predictive intervals are based on five draws per fold and provide a coarse rather than precise estimate of nominal 95% coverage. The 30 panels are also correlated across repeated splits. Their direction of failure is consistent, but interval coverage should be recalibrated in a larger confirmatory run before the simulator is used for prospective prediction.

## Conclusions

An empirically anchored multi-study rhizosphere 16S rRNA benchmark exposed scenario-specific failure boundaries that were not visible from a single method ranking. ANCOM-BC2 had the strongest overall FDP-power-F1 balance among the tested differential-abundance methods in the frozen grid, while LinDA showed reproducible FDP inflation under sparse, large effects. Core recovery was consistently vulnerable to depth confounding, synthetic direct-edge recovery was weak, and the tested small from-scratch neural classifiers underperformed established k-mer baselines. The repeated project-panel holdout provided only limited support for structural transportability across both generators. The benchmark therefore provides a framework for choosing sensitivity analyses and identifying method limits, while withholding claims of universal method superiority or reliable generalization to unseen rhizosphere projects.

## Methods

### Data resource and preprocessing

We assembled 45 public rhizosphere 16S projects from maize, rice and wheat, with 15 projects per crop. All projects were reprocessed from public FASTQ reads rather than combining published count tables. Raw reads from SRA/ENA were checked for file integrity and screened for the expected 16S marker before denoising. Amplicon length and primer orientation were measured for each project before choosing truncation parameters; a single default truncation length was not assumed. Reads were denoised with DADA2 [1], and taxonomic assignment used a SILVA reference [3]. Stringent marker-gene checks were applied to remove projects whose reads represented ITS, functional genes or mixed marker targets. Organelle reads were removed. SILVA placeholder names and reads not assignable below the required rank were collapsed into an unassigned category.

The feature space was mutually exclusive by construction. Each ASV was assigned to a genus label when an eligible genus was available; otherwise it was assigned to a single family-level fallback bin for that family. Family fallback bins were not added when the same reads already contributed to a genus bin, so parent-family and child-genus counts were not double counted. Reads assigned only to order, class or phylum, and SILVA placeholder labels such as “uncultured” or “metagenome,” were assigned to the single unassigned bin. The final matrix included 1,580 genus bins, 444 family fallback bins and one unassigned bin, totaling 2,025 features across 1,219 samples. For composition-based analysis and simulation-parameter estimation, samples required at least 1,000 bacterial reads, leaving 1,192 samples. Each project contributed at least four samples. The accession identifiers, sample counts and project-level sequencing summaries are listed in Table 1.

The unassigned bin was retained when constructing and normalizing the full sample-template composition, but it was excluded from differential-abundance testing and from all network nodes. Core and network feature panels were selected only from named genus or family bins. A sensitivity analysis recomputed all eight structural distances after removing the unassigned bin from both observed and synthetic matrices; all eight remained within their prespecified tolerances (Supplementary Table S6).

Taxonomy was cross-checked with two pipelines using different reference versions and decision conventions. Read-weighted genus concordance was 67.2%, and genus-level assignment rates differed by 16.2 percentage points. This discordance was retained as a limitation of taxonomic labels rather than resolved by post hoc majority calling.

### Benchmark architecture and simulation calibration

The benchmark distinguished four evidence components: observed project structure, simulation calibration, method evaluation against known ground truth and structural validation on held-out project panels (Fig. 1a). The eight structural summaries comprised feature-prevalence quantiles, log10 rank-abundance quantiles, log-depth quantiles, the ratio of within-project to between-project variance, pairwise Bray–Curtis quantiles, pairwise Aitchison quantiles, the fraction of zero entries and network topology. Network topology was summarized by the maximum of two Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances: one for project-level edge density and one for the coefficient of variation of node centrality.

For prevalence, rank abundance, depth, Bray–Curtis and Aitchison summaries, the distance was the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic between the observed and simulated sample distributions or quantile profiles. The variance-ratio distance was the absolute difference divided by the observed ratio. The zero-fraction distance was the maximum of the absolute scalar difference and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between sample zero fractions. Tolerances were 0.12, 0.12, 0.05, 0.30, 0.10, 0.10, 0.08 and 0.20 in the order above. They were fixed before the main simulation experiments and were treated as operational calibration criteria, not inferential null-hypothesis tests. The eight summaries are correlated, so the count passing tolerance is descriptive and is not interpreted as an eight-item independent validation score.

The primary generator used an empirical-Bayes project profile for each biological feature and an observed sample-template multinomial process. The sample template preserved joint zero patterns and sample-level covariation, while project profiles preserved between-project differences. A presence model related detection to abundance and sequencing depth. The project sample-size distribution and depth distribution were retained. The primary null generator passed all eight calibration summaries.

An independent project-mean plus presence-mask generator overestimated zeros and underestimated co-occurrence and was rejected before formal use. A correlated multiplicative perturbation of the calibrated template was used as generator 2. It changed feature relationships while retaining the common template, and is therefore described as semi-parametric rather than independent.

### Differential-abundance scenarios and methods

The formal DA grid contained five scenarios: no effect; 20 sparse targets with absolute log2 effect 0.25; 20 sparse targets with absolute effect 0.50; 20 sparse targets with absolute effect 1.00; and 40 dense targets with absolute effect 0.50. Each dataset contained 120 tested features from a fixed 15-project panel with five projects per crop. Conditions were assigned within project using a balanced seeded design, with at most one sample of imbalance per project.

Effects were injected into each sample template through positive feature multipliers, followed by compositional renormalization. The target estimand was the expected treatment/control log2 ratio in relative abundance after renormalization, not a change in unobserved absolute abundance. Multipliers were iterated until this expected post-renormalization effect was within 10^-5 log2 units of the requested effect. Closure-induced changes to non-target features were recorded in the truth table and were not redefined as biological target effects. The tested methods differ in their model-based null hypotheses and in whether they estimate model coefficients or compositional contrasts; method comparisons therefore combine empirical performance with estimand alignment.

Generator 1 used paired seeds 4000–4019, split into primary seeds 4000–4009 and extension seeds 4010–4019. The 100 datasets produced 400 method runs. Generator 2 used five paired seeds per scenario, producing 25 datasets and 100 method runs. Run status and failures remained in the denominator; formal generator-1 execution achieved 400/400 successful jobs.

Four methods were run through a unified R interface. ALDEx2 used project-condition pseudobulk aggregation, 128 Monte Carlo instances and a paired Welch contrast [8]. ANCOM-BC2 used condition as a fixed effect, project as a random effect, pseudo-count sensitivity and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment [9]. LinDA used CLR-transformed data, condition and project terms, a 0.10 prevalence filter, fixed pseudo-count 0.5 without adaptive adjustment, winsorisation of the upper 3% tail and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment [10]. MaAsLin2 used total-sum scaling, log transformation, a linear mixed model with condition fixed and project random, a 0.10 prevalence filter and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment [11].

The multiple-testing family was all tested features within one method, scenario and simulation replicate. Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment at q < 0.05 was primary [14]. Benjamini–Yekutieli was a prespecified stricter sensitivity analysis. For a single dataset, FDP was defined as V/max(R,1), where V was the number of rejected non-target features and R was the total number of rejections. FDP was undefined when R = 0. Across simulations, the mean of finite per-dataset FDP values was reported as a descriptive statistic. Pooled FDP was additionally calculated as total false discoveries divided by total discoveries (Supplementary Table S3); neither quantity was labeled as an empirical FDR without further assumptions. Power was the mean proportion of true target features rejected. F1 was the harmonic mean of precision and recall for target recovery. Method rankings were evaluated separately by scenario.

### Core and occupancy benchmark

The core simulator generated sample-feature detections from a logistic model with feature-level occupancy, project random effects and a fixed depth coefficient. For feature i, project j and sample s, the latent detection probability was

logit(p_ijs) = mu_i + b_ij + gamma x depth_z,js,

where b_ij was a project-level occupancy effect and depth_z,js was standardized log sequencing depth. A population-core feature had latent detection probability of at least 0.90 in at least 80% of projects. This truth was computed from latent probabilities and did not depend on a post hoc observed-prevalence cutoff.

Five scenarios were used: null, balanced core, low-abundance core, project-shift core and depth-confounded core. Each scenario contained 20 datasets, for 100 datasets and 400 method metrics. Calibration required exact depth preservation, non-zero sample depth, nuisance invariants and precomputed intervention-specific target envelopes. Ten stage-by-scenario calibration cells passed before method results were interpreted.

Four methods were evaluated: pooled sample prevalence, project-balanced prevalence, a beta-binomial empirical-Bayes model [18] and a hierarchical occupancy-detection model. Thresholds for probabilistic methods were selected on prespecified calibration seeds and frozen before formal evaluation. Primary endpoints were execution validity, false-core rate, core recall, F1 and Brier score.

### Network benchmark and real-panel stability

The synthetic network benchmark used 40 nodes, 15 projects and 20 samples per project. Log-normal depth, depth-dependent zeros, project-level latent shifts, compositional closure and multinomial sampling were included. True direct edges were encoded in a Gaussian precision matrix. Null, sparse, dense and project-shift scenarios were run with five calibration and five validation seeds.

Four estimators were compared: CLR Spearman correlation, CLR Pearson correlation, a SparCC-like iterative proportionality estimator and graphical lasso on the CLR covariance [12,13]. The SparCC-like implementation applied ten iterative proportionality updates to a CLR covariance matrix and is not the complete external SparCC software. The synthetic truth was the non-zero off-diagonal covariance structure encoded by a Gaussian precision matrix, which represents conditional dependence. Correlations and the SparCC-like estimator target marginal proportionality rather than the same conditional-dependence parameter, so their contrasts partly reflect estimand mismatch. Primary endpoints were edge AUPRC, edge AUROC and hub-degree Spearman correlation. There were 780 candidate undirected edges for 40 nodes. True-edge prevalence was computed for every seed, and the prevalence itself was used as the random-ranking AUPRC baseline (Supplementary Table S4). A universal top-10% edge cutoff was also evaluated in the null scenario.

Real-panel stability used three disjoint panels containing five maize, five rice and five wheat projects. Estimators were applied separately to each panel. Pairwise agreement was measured by top-decile edge Jaccard overlap, edge-score correlation and degree-rank correlation.

### Taxonomy-classification benchmark

The classification benchmark used SILVA SSU r138.2 V4 sequences corresponding to the 515F/806R region [3]. Eligible genera had at least ten full-length filtered V4 reference sequences. The 24 genera with the largest eligible sets were retained; four were withheld completely in each split and the remaining 20 formed the closed-set label space.

For each of five split seeds, six source sequences per genus were used for training, two for validation and two for testing. Training sequences were augmented threefold with 0.3% substitutions. Validation and test queries received 0.5% substitutions. The split seed controlled genus holdout and sequence assignment. Source sequences were disjoint across training, validation and test sets within each split, but the design did not cluster near-duplicate source sequences before assignment; this remains a risk to external validity.

Five classifiers were compared: a 4-mer/5-mer nearest-neighbor model, multinomial naive Bayes, linear SGD with log loss, a small one-dimensional CNN and a lightweight k-mer Transformer trained from scratch. The two neural models used four epochs, AdamW, a small parameter budget and no class weighting; because each split had 360 augmented training sequences and a batch size of 128, this corresponded to approximately 12 optimization steps. The unseen-genus rejection threshold was calibrated on validation queries to retain 95% of known taxa. The balanced majority-class baseline was 0.05 accuracy and 0.0025 macro F1. Closed-set accuracy and macro F1 were primary performance endpoints. The classification benchmark is a complementary reference-set analysis and was not included in the eight-summary structural calibration. The from-scratch Transformer is not a DNA-BERT model, and no DNA-BERT result is reported.

### Project-panel calibration and structural transportability

All 45 projects were divided into three disjoint 15-project panels with five projects per crop. Three seeds per panel and generator were used for structural calibration. This analysis tested whether the simulator reproduced panel summaries; it did not withhold composition from fitting.

Repeated structural transportability analysis used 10 stratified three-fold partitions. In each fold, 30 projects formed the training set and 15 projects formed the held-out panel. Held-out predictions used project count, crop and observed sequencing depth. Each held-out project was matched to one of the three nearest same-crop training projects using depth, modal ASV length, median ASV count and annotation composition. Counts for the held-out samples were generated without using their observed feature composition. Each project was held out once in every partition and therefore exactly 10 times per generator across the full procedure.

Two generators were evaluated. The fixed-tolerance endpoint counted how many of the eight structural summaries fell within the prespecified distances. A separate empirical-interval endpoint formed intervals from five predictive draws per fold and asked whether the observed held-out summary fell within the interval. For each metric and generator, coverage was the proportion of the 30 held-out panels whose observed value fell between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those five draws. The draws incorporated donor selection and multinomial count generation but did not propagate the full uncertainty of all fitted parameters. We therefore refer to this endpoint as empirical interval coverage rather than a precise 95% predictive interval because five draws provide limited resolution. The procedure evaluated whether generator-predicted structural summaries covered the held-out summaries; it did not evaluate the accuracy, FDP or ranking of differential-abundance, core or network methods on real held-out projects.

### Statistical reporting and reproducibility

Means are reported at the dataset, seed or fold level as stated. Standard deviations in the classification panel are across five split seeds. Network error bars are across five validation seeds. Method-level core F1 confidence intervals were generated by 5,000 fixed-seed bootstrap resamples of finite non-null method-by-dataset metrics (78–80 metrics per method). Differential-abundance 95% confidence intervals were obtained by 5,000 fixed-seed bootstrap resamples of datasets within each generator, scenario and method (Supplementary Table S3). Primary and extension stages were retained separately when describing replication. Because projects recur across repeated holdout partitions, panel-level results are reported descriptively without independent-sample confidence intervals or significance tests.

All scenario definitions, method thresholds and seed blocks were written before the formal runs. Command-line entry points, package versions and expected output schemas are preserved in the reproducibility materials. Figures were generated from machine-readable source-data tables. No result was selected on the basis of a preferred method ranking.

The analysis environment used R 4.5.3 with dada2 1.38.0, DECIPHER 3.6.0, ALDEx2 1.40.0, ANCOMBC 2.12.1, MicrobiomeStat 1.4 and Maaslin2 1.26.0. Numerical and machine-learning analyses used Python 3.12, NumPy 2.5.4, pandas 3.1.0, SciPy 1.18.1, scikit-learn 1.9.1 and PyTorch 2.14.1 (CPU build). The complete environment manifest and executable workflow are included in the analysis package.
